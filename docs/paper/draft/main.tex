\begin{abstract}
% Only measured statements; edit after the board session.
We describe a $\times 2$ image super-resolution accelerator for a ZedBoard (XC7Z020): a 2{,}620-parameter INT8 CNN upscales $960\times540$ to $1920\times1080$
in 1.312\,s per frame (0.762\,fps, 20-frame mean, board-measured) with 68 DSP48 blocks at 100\,MHz.
The output is byte-identical to a NumPy integer model on all 119 images of Set5, Set14 and BSD100 on the board.
On these sets the INT8 network gains about 1.4, 1.1 and 1.0\,dB PSNR-Y over bicubic interpolation (Table~\ref{tab:quality}).
On average only 17.2\,\% of the DSPs perform a MAC per cycle because the four layer engines run one after the other; a cycle model matches the simulated cycles of each layer to within 24 cycles, and its alternatives are labelled as predictions. Twenty injected RTL faults are all caught by the regression, five of them invisible to an image-quality comparison. The unchanged design fails timing at 152\,MHz; one added register closes 125\,MHz in Vivado (WNS +0.632\,ns), which is not yet tested on the board. Board energy and the quantization-aware variants are pending board runs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% Contributions must be re-checked AFTER the related-work reading (docs/paper/related_work_and_venues.md): layer-pipelined SR accelerators and bit-exact golden-model verification are NOT new ideas.
Image super-resolution turns a low-resolution picture into a larger one; small convolutional networks such as FSRCNN~\cite{dong2016fsrcnn} do this with far fewer parameters than the first deep models and are therefore candidates for low-cost embedded hardware.
This paper reports a complete, small implementation on a ZedBoard (Zynq XC7Z020) and, more than the accelerator itself, what was measured about it: how fast it is, why it is not faster, how reliably it is verified, and what limits its clock frequency.

The system upscales $960\times540$ RGB frames to $1920\times1080$ with a 2{,}620-parameter, four-layer network quantized to INT8. The network is trained and quantized on a PC, a NumPy integer model defines the exact result, a Verilog tile engine in the programmable logic reproduces it, and a bare-metal ARM program feeds it through an AXI DMA.
The deliberate scope is a design that can be checked completely, not the highest throughput; the numbers below show what that choice costs.

Our contributions are measurements and analyses of one design:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A board-verified INT8 accelerator.} Output on the ZedBoard is byte-identical to the integer model on all 119 images of Set5, Set14 and BSD100; a frame takes 1.312\,s (0.762\,fps, 20-frame mean) with 68 DSP48 blocks at 100\,MHz (Sections~\ref{sec:arch} and~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{A quantified utilization gap.} On average only 17.2\,\% of the 68 DSPs perform a useful MAC per cycle, because the four layer engines run one after the other. A cycle model reproduces the simulated per-layer cycle counts to within 17--24 cycles per 64$\times$64 tile, and is then used, as a clearly labelled prediction, to estimate alternative organisations (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\item \textbf{A measured verification strength.} Twenty hand-written single-edit faults in the datapath and control RTL are all detected by the quick regression; five of them change the image quality by less than 0.01\,dB and would be invisible to a PSNR comparison (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\item \textbf{A localized negative timing result.} The unchanged design does not close timing at 152\,MHz (WNS $-1.693$\,ns); 434 of 580 failing endpoints are one path type, and a one-register change is analysed (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}
Two further network variants (quantization-aware training and a nearest-neighbour anchor) are simulated bit-exactly but have not yet run on the board; they are reported as such (Section~\ref{sec:quant}).

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}
The network (\texttt{software/\allowbreak{}ai/\allowbreak{}models/\allowbreak{}srnet.py}) maps an RGB image to a $2\times$ larger one with four convolutions and a pixel shuffle:
$3\times3$ convolution $3\!\to\!16$ with ReLU, $3\times3$ depthwise convolution (16 channels) with ReLU, $1\times1$ convolution $16\!\to\!16$ with ReLU,
$3\times3$ convolution $16\!\to\!12$, and a pixel shuffle with factor 2 (channel $k=4c+2\,dy+dx$).
All convolutions are valid (no padding): the input carries a 3-pixel border (replicated for whole images, real neighbours for tiles).
It has 2{,}620 parameters and 2{,}560 multiply-accumulates (MACs) per input pixel (asserted in \texttt{software/\allowbreak{}ai/\allowbreak{}evaluation/\allowbreak{}test\_model.py}).

\section{Quantization}\label{sec:quant}
Activations are unsigned 8-bit with one scale per layer, weights are signed 8-bit with a per-layer scale (values in $[-127,127]$), biases and accumulators are 32-bit.
A layer output is $y=\mathrm{clamp}\big((acc\cdot M+2^{S-1})\gg S,\,0,\,255\big)$ with a 16-bit multiplier $M$ per output channel and a shift $S$ per layer
($S=24,23,21,24$ for L1--L4); the product $acc\cdot M$ needs about 40 bits.
The activation range of each layer is a percentile of all post-ReLU values over 100 training crops.
The percentile was chosen on 12 validation images (Table~\ref{tab:ptq}); post-training quantization (PTQ) is sufficient, quantization-aware training was not needed for the deployed network.

\subsection{Hardware mapping of the requantizer}
In hardware the requantization of one output value is a single DSP48E1 operation: the 32-bit accumulator times the 16-bit multiplier gives a signed product that needs about 40 bits (the DSP has 48), the rounding constant $2^{S-1}$ is added, the result is shifted right by $S$ and clamped to $[0,255]$.
The requantizer is checked on its own with vectors for shifts 1, 2, 3, 8, 21, 23 and 24 and with randomized layers in which every output channel has a different multiplier (\texttt{hardware/\allowbreak{}verification}, \texttt{gen\_unit\_vectors.py}). A deliberately wrong shared multiplier is detected only by the randomized layers (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), because the deployed weights give all channels of a layer the same $M$.
For new weight sets an overflow analysis is part of the export: for the quantization-aware candidate R2q the largest possible $|acc\cdot M|$ plus rounding needs at most 35 bits (limit 48), and the accumulators keep 13--15 bits of headroom below 32 bits even under the worst-case bound (\texttt{results/\allowbreak{}quality/\allowbreak{}acc\_range\_R2q\_rep\_qat\_s1.txt}).

\subsection{Quantization-aware candidates (integer model; not yet on the board)}
Post-training quantization is enough for the deployed network, but not for every network. A re-parameterized network (training-time blocks merged into plain convolutions) collapses under PTQ, and quantization-aware training (QAT) recovers $+3.40$, $+1.99$ and $+1.54$\,dB PSNR-Y on Set5, Set14 and BSD100 (R2q $-$ R2, mean of two seeds; \texttt{results/\allowbreak{}quality/\allowbreak{}image\_quality\_ladder.md}).
Against the mean of two trainings of the deployed recipe (R0), the QAT network R2q gains $+0.87$, $+0.50$ and $+0.36$\,dB, and R3q, which adds a nearest-neighbour anchor (the upscaled input is added to the last layer's output), $+1.12$, $+0.70$ and $+0.49$\,dB; R3q is $+0.26$, $+0.20$ and $+0.13$\,dB better than R2q (two seeds each, integer model, selection on validation images only).
R2q needs no RTL change except different constants (shifts 22, 22, 21, 22); the anchor needs a small RTL change (the anchor pixel is added through the requantizer's rounding constant, no extra cycle; \texttt{results/\allowbreak{}utilization/\allowbreak{}m6\_system\_R3q.md} in the sandbox branch \texttt{r3q}).
Both are simulated and netlist-simulated bit-exactly but \emph{pending board run}; every board statement in this paper refers to the deployed design R0.

\section{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}
% Sources for every number in this section are named in the comments; board numbers are from results/speed/*.md, simulation numbers from results/utilization/m4_*.md, m5_*.md, m6_*.md.
The accelerator runs on a ZedBoard (Zynq XC7Z020-CLG484, 220 DSP48E1, 140 RAMB36). The processing system (PS, dual ARM Cortex-A9) runs a bare-metal driver; the programmable logic (PL) holds an AXI DMA, an AXI4-Lite register block and the tile core.
Everything in the PL uses one clock, \texttt{FCLK\_CLK0}, at 100\,MHz, so there is no clock-domain crossing.

\subsection{Tiling and dataflow}
The frame is processed in tiles of $64\times64$ input pixels (producing $128\times128$ output pixels). Because all convolutions are valid, each tile needs a 3-pixel halo: $70\times70\times3 = 14{,}700$ bytes go in and
$128\times128\times3 = 49{,}152$ bytes come out per tile. The ARM pads the $960\times540$ frame by replication, which gives $15\times9 = 135$ tiles per frame; the halo makes the engines compute about 10\,\% more MACs than the untiled frame (1.460 vs.\ 1.327\,G; cycle model, \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}paper/\allowbreak{}cycle\_model.md}).
A tile is processed in four steps that do not overlap: (i) the ARM copies the 70$\times$70 patch to a DDR buffer and the DMA streams it into the core, (ii) the four convolution layers run one after the other, (iii) the pixel-shuffled result streams back through the DMA to a second buffer, and (iv) the ARM places it in the output frame.
The ARM work (padding 5.9\,ms and tile copies 26.4\,ms per frame) is measured on the board (Table~\ref{tab:system} and \texttt{results/\allowbreak{}speed/\allowbreak{}m7\_timing\_20frames.md}).

\subsection{Tile core and convolution engines}
The tile core (\texttt{sr\_tile\_core.v}) contains an input loader, five tile RAMs, four convolution engines, a pixel shuffle and an output packer.
Each activation RAM stores one pixel per word with the channels side by side (word widths 24, 128, 128, 128 and 96 bits for the input and the outputs of L1--L4); the engines read and write them directly.
There is one engine per layer and one RAM per layer boundary (no ping-pong); a small sequencer starts the engines one after the other.
An engine produces one output pixel every \textit{PERIOD} = max(\textit{steps}, $C_\mathrm{out}$) cycles, where \textit{steps} is the number of kernel taps times input channels: 27, 16, 16 and 144 cycles per pixel for L1--L4 (measured in simulation).
It has one MAC lane per output channel, built as a DSP48E1 (uint8 activation $\times$ int8 weight, product register, 32-bit accumulator), a weight ROM that delivers all lanes' weights for the current step, and one requantizer shared by all channels that runs in the shadow of the next pixel's MACs.
The lanes plus two requantizer DSPs give 18, 18, 18 and 14 DSP48 blocks, 68 in total (31\,\% of the device).
The schedule is data independent: a 64$\times$64 tile takes 124{,}872, 69{,}713, 69{,}720 and 589{,}844 cycles in the four engines (854{,}149 together) and 926{,}201 cycles for the whole tile in simulation, identical on all nine real-size golden tiles; the board counter reads 926{,}217--926{,}241 (mean 926{,}220) per tile.
The difference of 72{,}052 cycles between the tile and the engines is byte-wide stream I/O (14{,}700 cycles to load, 49{,}152 bytes out, and fetch cycles; derived by subtraction).
% sources: results/utilization/m4_*.md, m5_tile_core.md, docs/paper/cycle_model.md Part 1 (simulated column), results/speed/m7_timing_20frames.md (board CYCLES).
The layer RAMs dominate the memory: a plain $4624\times128$-bit RAM maps to 32 RAMB36 because Vivado rounds the depth to a power of two, so the RAMs are split into a power-of-two part plus a remainder (18.5 RAMB36 for each 128-bit RAM). The whole core uses 70 of 140 block-RAM tiles and 68 of 220 DSPs
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak{}utilization/\allowbreak{}m5\_tile\_core.md}); in the complete system (PS7, DMA, interconnect, our logic) the 100\,MHz build uses 5{,}296 LUT, 7{,}831 FF, 72 BRAM tiles and 68 DSP with WNS +0.503\,ns (Table~\ref{tab:system}).
One register stage between the engine's result mux and the requantizer (added after a first timing run) costs one cycle per engine run and nothing in throughput.

\subsection{AXI interface, registers and driver}
The wrapper (\texttt{sr\_axis\_wrapper.v}) converts a 32-bit AXI4-Stream (little-endian) into the core's byte stream and packs the output bytes into 32-bit beats with \texttt{TLAST} on the last one. It accepts input only during the core's load phase, so the DMA can be armed before the start pulse.
It flags an early \texttt{TLAST}, a missing \texttt{TLAST} on the last input beat and a start while busy; the top module adds a \texttt{TKEEP} check. The first error of a tile is kept in \texttt{ERROR\_CODE}.
The AXI4-Lite block (\texttt{sr\_accel\_top.v}) has CONTROL, STATUS, TILE\_SIZE, the core's CYCLES counter, ERROR\_CODE, VERSION and seven read-only debug registers (\texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}REGISTER\_MAP.md}).
The driver programs the DMA in simple mode, polls with a timeout of about one second, and on any error resets the core, then the DMA, then the core again before it checks that STATUS and ERROR\_CODE are clear. It never retries a failed tile silently.
On the board this was exercised with deliberate faults (input too short, input too long, output buffer too short) and with 8{,}000 back-to-back initialisation-and-tile cycles without an error (\texttt{results/\allowbreak{}speed/\allowbreak{}m6\_board.md}, tests T5 and T6).
The early first-tile failures of 2026-10-11 were traced to reprogramming the PL while a DMA transfer was in flight; the programming scripts now isolate the PL first. Two single failures remain unexplained (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Verification chain}
The reference chain is PyTorch FP32 $\to$ NumPy integer model $\to$ RTL, and the RTL is compared with the integer model byte for byte. The regression (\texttt{hardware/\allowbreak{}verification/\allowbreak{}run\_tests.sh full}, Icarus Verilog, 88 runs) checks every engine layer by layer against golden tile dumps, randomized layers with distinct multipliers per channel, the whole core at the real tile size, a 4-tile seam test, the AXI wrapper and the register block (with random stalls), and one-cycle-reset aborts; post-synthesis netlists are simulated in xsim.
On the board, the output of all 119 test images (Set5, Set14, BSD100) is byte-identical to the integer model (\texttt{results/\allowbreak{}quality/\allowbreak{}m7\_board\_testsets\_run.log}).
How well this regression detects bugs is measured in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% Every number is from a stored file named in a comment; predictions are labelled "predicted"/"model" in the text.

\subsection{Why 0.76\,fps: the utilization gap}
The design is slow for a structural reason, and the reason can be computed. A frame needs 1.327\,G MACs (2{,}560 per input pixel), 1.460\,G as tiled. At 68 DSPs and 100\,MHz the ideal time would be 0.195\,s; the board needs 1.312\,s.
Inside the tile core the average is 11.7 MACs per cycle, \textbf{17.2\,\%} of the 68 DSPs, and over the whole frame including the ARM and DMA part 11.1 MACs per cycle (16.4\,\%).
The cause is not idle lanes inside an engine (lane utilization is 100\,\% in L1, L3 and L4 and 56\,\% in L2) but that only one of the four engines runs at a time, so on average about three quarters of the DSPs wait.
Layer 4 alone takes 589{,}844 of the 926{,}201 cycles of a tile (63.7\,\%): it has 144 steps per output pixel and only 12 lanes.
The core itself accounts for 95.3\,\% of the frame time (1{,}250.4 of 1{,}311.9\,ms); the ARM and DMA overhead is 61.5\,ms (\texttt{results/\allowbreak{}speed/\allowbreak{}m7\_timing\_20frames.md}, \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}paper/\allowbreak{}cycle\_model.md} Part~1).
The cycle model reproduces the simulated cycles of each layer to within 17 to 24 cycles per tile (+24, +17, +24, +20 for L1--L4), so it is a fair basis for the predictions below, but the predictions themselves are \emph{not} measurements.

\textbf{Predictions (model only, nothing built).} A line-buffer pipeline in which all four layers work at once, with the same 68 DSPs, would need about 25.2\,M cycles per frame, i.e.\ \emph{predicted} 258\,ms (3.9\,fps) at 100\,MHz, assuming no stalls, a 32-bit output stream and no tile halo (Part~2 of the cycle model).
A single shared engine that uses all DSPs for one layer at a time would be \emph{predicted} at 2.2\,fps (I/O as today) or 2.8\,fps (I/O overlapped) at 100\,MHz (Part~3). Such figures omit stalls and control cost, and layer-pipelined designs already exist in the literature (to be cited in the related-work section), so we claim the measured gap and its cause, not the pipeline as a contribution.

\subsection{What limits the clock}
The unchanged design does not close timing at 152.38\,MHz (the nearest frequency the PS7 PLL gave to a 150\,MHz request): worst negative slack $-1.693$\,ns, total negative slack $-421.053$\,ns, 580 of 23{,}943 endpoints failing, while the 100\,MHz board build has WNS $+0.503$\,ns (\texttt{results/\allowbreak{}utilization/\allowbreak{}timing\_150mhz/\allowbreak{}}, \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}paper/\allowbreak{}timing\_150mhz.md}; the run was repeated and every number reproduced).
The failures are concentrated: 434 endpoints (worst $-1.693$\,ns) are one path type, an activation RAM $\to$ channel-select multiplexer $\to$ the A input of a MAC DSP48E1, which has no input register in the current RTL and therefore needs 1.928\,ns of setup against 0.362\,ns for an input with its own register.
The next classes are inside the Xilinx AXI DMA (106 endpoints, worst $-0.885$\,ns), the requantizer feed (21, $-0.392$\,ns), our AXI-Lite registers (9, $-0.174$\,ns), the weight ROM $\to$ DSP B input (8, $-0.153$\,ns) and the last RAM $\to$ output word (2, $-0.091$\,ns).
A one-register change was derived from these paths (\texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}paper/\allowbreak{}fmax\_plan.md}): the activation is registered once more before the MAC, and the weight address and control signals follow one cycle later. The register costs one cycle per engine run, i.e.\ 4 cycles per tile: the tile core takes 926{,}205 instead of 926{,}201 cycles in simulation (the same real-size golden tiles in both trees; \texttt{results/\allowbreak{}utilization/\allowbreak{}r0\_125\_sim\_cycles.txt} in branch \texttt{r0\_125}). Whether Vivado places the register inside the DSP's own input register does not change this count; it changes only the setup time at that pin.
With this change the whole system was rebuilt at 125.000\,MHz (branch \texttt{r0\_125}, \texttt{results/\allowbreak{}utilization/\allowbreak{}m6\_system\_R0\_125.md} in that branch): post-route WNS $+0.632$\,ns, WHS $+0.032$\,ns, no failing endpoint of 24{,}167, 5{,}251 LUT, 7{,}979 FF (+148), 72 BRAM tiles and 68 DSPs, and the full regression (88 runs) passes.
The plan had estimated about $+1.3$\,ns for the worst RAM-to-MAC path; the built design has $+0.63$\,ns on the same path type, so the estimate was optimistic by about 0.7\,ns because the placement changed. 43 of the 60 MAC DSPs use their internal input register; the other 17 keep a fabric register.
At 150\,MHz the same single register is not enough by the plan's arithmetic (about $-0.02$\,ns for the RAM path and about $-0.8$\,ns in the DMA IP), and this was not built.
The \emph{predicted} frame time at 125\,MHz, with the ARM and DMA overhead held at 61.5\,ms, is 1{,}061.8\,ms (0.942\,fps; \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}paper/\allowbreak{}cycle\_model.md} Part~4); the 125\,MHz bitstream is \emph{pending board run}, and board power at the higher clock is not measured.

\subsection{How strong is the verification?}
A bit-exact regression passes if the design is right, but it says little about whether it would fail if the design were wrong. We therefore injected 20 single-edit faults into the RTL (7 in the requantizer, 4 in the MAC, 3 in the engine, 3 in the tile core, 3 in the control path) and ran the quick regression (36 runs) on each; an unmutated control passes 36 of 36.
\textbf{All 20 are detected; none survives} (\texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}paper/\allowbreak{}mutation\_study.md}). The result is more informative than the count. Five faults change the output quality by less than 0.01\,dB PSNR-Y on the 119 test images and would pass any quality comparison: a bias that is one too large (A3, $+0.0020$\,dB mean), a requantizer valid-pipeline reset gate (K1), a busy flag that is not reset (K2), a cycle counter that is not reset (K3), and a bug that gives every channel the multiplier of channel~0 (E1).
E1 changes no output at all on the deployed weights, because all channels of a layer share one multiplier. These faults are seen only by specific tests: E1 only by the randomized layers with distinct multipliers per channel, K1 and K2 only by the reset and abort sweeps of the conv-engine sections, K3 by the wrapper, core and seam sections. Removing any one of these tests would let a fault survive.
The limits are as important: the mutants are hand-written and sit in the requantizer, MAC, engine and tile core, not in the AXI wrapper, the top module, the weight ROM or the split RAM; 18 of the 20 image-quality columns rest on a Python twin that reproduced the RTL output byte for byte (C3 is not verified, E3 has no twin); only the quick suite was run on the mutants (the full suite contains it); and everything is simulation, not board.

\subsection{Limits}
0.76\,fps is not real time. Load, compute and output of a tile are not overlapped. The power figure in Table~\ref{tab:system} is a Vivado estimate with low confidence; a board-level measurement (12\,V input, multimeter on the current-sense resistor) is \emph{pending board run}, and it will not be comparable to the on-chip estimate.
The timing run uses one image and one board; the ARM-only baseline (T7) and the R2q and R3q candidates are \emph{pending board run}.
Images go in over JTAG, not from a video source. Two single first-tile failures on 2026-10-11 remain unexplained (the 8{,}000-tile test afterwards showed none; \texttt{results/\allowbreak{}speed/\allowbreak{}m6\_board.md}).
No comparison with other FPGA super-resolution results is made here (related work is still to be written).
